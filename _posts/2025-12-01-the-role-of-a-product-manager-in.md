<p>Three months ago, <a href="https://yongqianme.substack.com/p/from-founder-to-employee-breaking">I stepped away from my startups</a>, closed all my past projects, and decided to work for a company.</p>
<p>Back in April, <a href="https://qianyong.me/posts/2025/04/Why-I-Walked-Away-from-a-%247-Million-Robotics-Deal/">I walked away from a $7M robotics deal</a> because the technology simply wasn’t ready for mining exploration—it still isn’t even today. However, that project gave me valuable insight into <strong>embodied AI</strong>, which led me to join an embodied AI startup.</p>
<p>In the weeks after joining, I quickly realized that the role of a <strong>product manager in embodied AI</strong> is very different from a typical PM who works only in software or hardware. It’s a huge challenge—and one worth sharing. So I wrote this short article to illustrate what makes a <strong>good PM vs. a bad PM</strong> in embodied AI. I’d like to share it with all of you.</p>
<h2>The Role of a Product Manager in Embodied Intelligence</h2>
<p>In the era of embodied intelligence, a product manager’s role goes far beyond writing requirements or aligning schedules. A truly great PM can <strong>bridge AI, hardware, software, and real-world deployment</strong>.</p>
<p>To create embodied robots that work reliably in the real world, a PM must be able to:</p>
<ul>
<li><p><strong>Guide the team with a clear vision</strong></p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Stay decisive in complex hardware-software collaboration</strong></p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Turn real customer pain points into engineering and product priorities</strong></p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Drive results even in uncertain environments</strong></p>
</li>

</ul>
<p>This guide—<strong>“Good PM / Bad PM”</strong>—aims to align the team on the responsibilities, value, and boundaries of this role.</p>
<p>We also encourage the team to:</p>
<ul>
<li><p><strong>Hold PMs accountable</strong>—speak up when necessary</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Point out when PMs are not demonstrating the “good” behaviors</strong></p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Support and trust PMs when they deliver</strong></p>
</li>

</ul>
<blockquote><p><strong>Remember: a great product is always a team effort.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<h2>Good PM vs. Bad PM</h2>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Vision &amp; Mission Alignment</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Makes decisions around the core mission (real-world deployment), translates strategic vision into concrete goals, focuses on <strong>deployment</strong> over flashy demos.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Chases trends, lacks strategic judgment, substitutes slogans for action plans.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Execution &amp; Accountability</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Takes responsibility for results, not just process; aligns the team with clear, verifiable PRDs; distinguishes between demo and mass production.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Shifts blame, provides vague documentation, fails to drive cross-team problem-solving.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Customer &amp; Market Understanding</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Understands real-world scenarios (warehouse, factory, retail, hospital), identifies real pain points (reliability, safety, efficiency), and validates continuously in the field.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Works in isolation to impress investors, ignores customer feedback, focuses on flashy tech instead of real needs.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Cross-Team Collaboration</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Speaks both engineering and business languages, proactively builds shared understanding, turns disagreements into consensus.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Pushes work onto teams, thinks sending documents equals collaboration, lacks alignment mechanisms.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Prioritization &amp; Focus</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Focuses on high-value problems, dares to say “no,” delays low-priority projects, emphasizes <strong>impact over quantity</strong>.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Says yes to everything, confuses priorities, spreads resources thin, producing little.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Communication &amp; Clarity</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Keeps information transparent, explains complex technical and business trade-offs clearly, ensures everyone understands <strong>why</strong>.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Hides behind jargon or slides, communicates results without reasoning, is passive in communication.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Data &amp; Evidence</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Uses core metrics (stability, task success rate, MTBF) to drive iterations, actively collects and validates data, and uses data to tell a story.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Acts on gut feeling, doesn’t track KPIs, believes AI will “improve itself.”</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Speed &amp; Resilience</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Moves fast without sacrificing quality, removes obstacles daily, and has backup plans for hardware challenges.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Waits for perfection, delays addressing problems, underestimates the complexity of software-hardware integration.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Leadership &amp; Culture</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Energizes the team, builds influence through trust, transparency, and results, and upholds product integrity.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Plays politics, shifts blame, avoids responsibility, pursues personal credit.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

<li>
<p><strong>Long-Term Thinking</strong></p>

<ul>
<li><p><strong>Good PM:</strong> Designs systems, not temporary features; adopts a platform perspective (SDKs, data flywheels, services); balances current delivery with future scalability.</p>
</li>

<li><p><strong>Bad PM:</strong> Focuses only on short-term KPIs, accumulates technical debt, treats the roadmap as a wish list.</p>
</li>

</ul>

</li>

</ol>
<blockquote><p>A great product is built by a team that shares <strong>vision, trust, and accountability</strong>.</p>
</blockquote>
